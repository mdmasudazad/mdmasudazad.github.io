Abstract

Steel-reinforced elastomeric bearings work, but they price ordinary low-rise construction out of seismic isolation — especially in developing countries. Scrap tire rubber pad (STRP) isolators are a low-cost alternative built from discarded tires, but every change to geometry, material, or loading condition has traditionally meant another physical test or another full finite-element run. This study replaces that loop: 350 FE models of bonded and unbonded STRP isolators were generated under combined compression and cyclic shear, yielding 2,089 labeled data points, and six machine learning models were trained to predict horizontal stiffness directly from geometry, material, and boundary-condition inputs — no destructive testing required.

Chapter One

Introduction & Motivation

Earthquake damage in low-and-mid-rise buildings is still a serious problem in developing countries, where the cost of conventional steel-reinforced elastomeric bearings puts seismic isolation out of reach for ordinary residential and rural construction. Scrap tire rubber pad (STRP) isolators close that gap: tires already combine high vertical stiffness with significant horizontal flexibility, thanks to their vulcanized rubber and steel-cord construction — and reusing them turns a waste-disposal problem into a seismic-safety solution.

Prior experimental and FE work has shown that STRP isolators depend heavily on geometry, tire type, surface pressure, and displacement amplitude, and that the bonding condition at the interface — bonded (BSTRP) versus unbonded (UBSTRP) — fundamentally changes how the isolator deforms. Unbonded isolators can roll over at the edges under large shear, which changes their stiffness behavior in ways that are hard to capture with simple formulas.

The bottleneck is design iteration: every new geometry or material combination has historically required a fresh physical test or a full nonlinear FE simulation just to extract a stiffness value. That's slow, expensive, and sometimes destructive. This study proposes a machine-learning shortcut, trained on a large FE-generated dataset, so engineers can estimate horizontal stiffness directly from known parameters.

Three objectives

  1. Quantify how geometry, material parameters, and boundary conditions drive STRP horizontal stiffness.
  2. Show that ML models can accurately predict that stiffness from FE-generated data.
  3. Provide a practical pathway for preliminary design and optimization of low-cost STRP isolation systems.

Chapter Two

Finite Element Modeling & Validation

All FE analysis was run in MSC Marc Mentat on square 100 mm × 100 mm STRP isolators, built from four bonded 12 mm tire stacks for a total height of 48 mm. Tread pattern and tire curvature were simplified away; what mattered was the layered rubber-and-steel-cord composite underneath.

Material constitutive models

Rubber was meshed with 8-node solid, full-integration Hermann elements — well suited to near-incompressible material without volumetric locking — and given a three-term Mooney–Rivlin hyperelastic law:

W = C10(I1−3) + C01(I2−3) + C11(I1−3)(I2−3)

A Prony series captured time-dependent viscoelasticity, and a Mullins-effect damage model captured stress softening under repeated cycles. Steel cords — negligible in bending, strong in tension — were modeled as four-node membrane elements embedded in the rubber host material.

Boundary conditions & loading

Rigid top and bottom plates stood in for the superstructure and substructure. A constant 5 MPa axial pressure represented service-level compression, after which cyclic lateral loading was applied up to 250% shear strain. Bonded contact was fully glued; unbonded contact used a bilinear Coulomb friction model (μ = 0.8) that allows separation — and the rollover behavior that makes unbonded isolators interesting.

Validation against experiment

The FE model was checked against Mishra's (2012) experimental STRP-4 specimen. Force–displacement loops matched closely, and the two tables below — horizontal stiffness and effective damping across four displacement levels — stayed within roughly 20% of each other, close enough to trust the model for the parametric sweep that follows.

Chapter Three

Parametric Study

With the FE model validated, every geometric, material, and boundary-condition variable gets its own sweep. Each card below is one variable, isolated, on the 72 mm × 72 mm × 24 mm reference isolator unless noted otherwise.

Boundary condition

Bonded vs. unbonded

BSTRP stiffness is high across the whole strain range and dips only slightly before climbing again — from 348.4 N/mm at 25% strain down to 326.8 N/mm at 100%, then up to 487.1 N/mm at 250%. Unbonded (UBSTRP) softens further, bottoming out at 214.7 N/mm at 150% strain, before full-rollover — the isolator's edges touching the ground — pushes stiffness back up to 369.7 N/mm at 250%.

Boundary condition

Axial pressure

Bonded isolators barely notice axial pressure — a 2 to 12 MPa sweep lifts Kh only ~2%. Unbonded isolators are the opposite story at large strain: at 250% shear, Kh jumps from 166.9 to 453.8 N/mm across the same pressure range, because higher axial load suppresses the sliding that would otherwise bleed off stiffness after rollover.

Boundary condition

Friction coefficient

Friction (μ = 0.6–1.2) barely registers below 150% shear strain — but once full-rollover kicks in, it matters a lot: at 250% strain Kh rises from 313.7 to 394.8 N/mm as μ climbs, because more friction resists sliding at the new rollover contact patch.

Loading condition

Loading rate

Rubber is viscoelastic, so faster loading means stiffer response. Bonded Kh at 100% strain climbs from 272.7 N/mm at R = 0.67 s⁻¹ to 419.4 N/mm at R = 3.0 s⁻¹ — a 53% swing driven purely by how much time the rubber's molecular chains get to relax.

Material property

Hyperelastic constants (C₁₀, C₀₁, C₁₁)

C₁₀ and C₀₁ govern the initial shear modulus and lift stiffness at low-to-moderate strain. C₁₁ is the strain-hardening term — it does almost nothing at small strain, then dominates at large strain: at 200% shear, BSTRP stiffness rises from 283.2 to 507.2 N/mm as C₁₁ goes from 0.05 to 0.3 MPa, an ~80% increase.

Material property

Prony viscoelastic terms

Longer relaxation times (τ₁, τ₂) raise Kh — stress just sticks around longer. Deviatoric energy multipliers (E₁, E₂) do the opposite: pushing E₁ from 0.1 to 0.5 drops BSTRP stiffness from 582.8 to 392.3 N/mm, since a larger multiplier means more of the instantaneous modulus is available to relax away.

Material property

Mullins damage parameters

Scalar factors SF₁, SF₂, and SF∞ scale stiffness directly and strongly — SF∞ from 0.3 to 0.8 more than doubles BSTRP stiffness at 250% strain (296.6 → 728.9 N/mm). The relaxation parameters (RP₁, RP₂), by contrast, barely move Kh at all — they shape how damage evolves, not how much stiffness it removes.

Material property

Steel cord Young's modulus

Sweeping the steel reinforcement from 50,000 to 250,000 MPa changes Kh by less than 1% in most cases — in-plane shear response is governed by the rubber, not the cords. A small dependence appears only at large shear strain in unbonded isolators, once rollover engages the cords in combined bending and axial action.

Geometry

Plan area & total height

Geometry moves Kh more than anything else in the study. Holding height constant and growing the plan from 50×50 mm to 200×200 mm takes unbonded stiffness from 224 to 2,857 N/mm at 25% strain — over an order of magnitude — because Kh scales with the effective shear area (GA/H). Going the other way, taller isolators are floppier: holding the 150×150 mm plan fixed and stretching height from 24 to 72 mm cuts stiffness by roughly 70%, tracking the classical Kh ∝ 1/H relationship. Plan area sets the isolator's load capacity; height sets its flexibility — and a real design has to balance both.

Machine Learning Program

The parametric sweep produced 2,089 data points from 350 unique FE models, spanning geometry, material behavior, and loading condition. That dataset was split 80/20 — 1,671 training samples, 418 held out for testing — and used to train six well-established supervised learning models, deliberately spanning simple and ensemble methods:

Tuning

Grid search handled the cheaper models (RF, KNN); XGBoost and Gradient Boosting were tuned manually against literature-informed starting points, balancing accuracy against training cost. XGBoost's final configuration: 500 estimators, learning rate 0.05, max depth 5, min child weight 5, column subsample 0.8 — chosen via 10-fold cross-validation to guard against overfitting.

Evaluation metrics

Chapter Five

Results & Discussion

XGBoost led every metric in both phases — R² = 0.998 training / 0.983 testing, with the lowest RMSE and MAE of any model. Gradient Boosting followed at a clear second. KNN, Random Forest, and Ridge Regression trailed, with KNN in particular showing the classic overfitting signature: strong on training data, weaker on the held-out set.

What drives the prediction

Aspect ratio (length ÷ height) is the single most influential feature across every model — sometimes by a wide margin, as in AdaBoost, where it accounts for nearly 0.7 of total importance. XGBoost balances it against total tire stacks and isolator length; Ridge Regression spreads importance more evenly across aspect ratio, shape factor, and length.

Chapter Six

Closing Notes

Geometry rules

Plan area and total height dominate horizontal stiffness — aspect ratio alone explains most of what the ML models learn.

Bonding is a mode switch

Bonded and unbonded isolators don't just differ in magnitude — they follow fundamentally different deformation mechanisms, especially once rollover engages.

XGBoost generalizes best

Across every metric and both phases, gradient-boosted trees with careful tuning outperformed simpler and more complex alternatives alike.

A practical design tool

A trained model this accurate turns stiffness estimation into a lookup, not a simulation — real leverage for low-cost seismic isolation in resource-limited settings.
